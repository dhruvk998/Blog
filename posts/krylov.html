Quantum Chaos

Krylov Complexity

June 2026 12 min read

Background

How do quantum operators grow? If you evolve an operator in time through a Hamiltonian, one is often interested in characterizing how complex this evolution is. Are there any patterns of growth we can find? Quantum dynamics, with its vast richness, has recently benefitted from adopting a rather old perspective on matrices and linear algebra, dating back to the 1930s. In this article, I seek to elucidate the so-called Krylov subspace methods in the context of quantum mechanics. We will see that these techniques allow us to quantify the Krylov complexity of the evolution of an operator. With wide applications in fields ranging from open quantum systems to holography, Krylov complexity has found to provide deep insights and shows consistent universality across all regimes of dynamics, from the integrable to the completely chaotic.

My goal is to leave you with a concrete understanding of the ideas I mention, and to keep it accessible I may gloss over some gory details. But for the interested reader, I have added drop downs wherever you may want some more clarity. This way, I hope to cater to a larger audience.

Schrödinger's equation states \(i \partial_t |\psi \rangle=\hat{H}|\psi \rangle\) and is the governing equation for the evolution of quantum states \(|\psi \rangle\) under a Hamiltonian \(\hat{H}\). For a time independent Hamiltonian, the solutions can be formally written as \(|\psi(t)\rangle=\hat{U}|\psi(0)\rangle\) where \(\hat{U}= \exp(-i\hat{H}t)\). Furthermore, the expectation value of any observable \(\hat{O}\) varies with time, and this naturally brings us to the Heisenberg picture▸, where we fix the states and evolve the operators in time instead. Both methods yield the same answers, but it may be helpful to sometimes work in the Heisenberg picture. In this article, we will find it to be immensely useful.

From Schrödinger to Heisenberg close ✕

The expectation value of any observable \(\hat{O}\) varies with time and is given as

\[ \langle\psi(t)|\hat{O}|\psi(t)\rangle=\langle\psi(0)| e^{i\hat{H}t} \hat{O} e^{-i\hat{H}t}|\psi(0)\rangle \equiv \langle\psi(0)|\hat{O}(t)|\psi(0)\rangle. \]

The last equivalence brings us to the Heisenberg picture, where we fix the states and evolve the operators in time as

\[ \hat{O}(t)=e^{i\hat{H}t} \hat{O} e^{-i\hat{H}t} \]

instead.

Vectorization

Before we begin, I just want to give a heads up on the idea of vectorization▸, which will be central to our discussion. As mentioned, those familiar with it may skip ahead, noting that I would be using the row-vectorization convention.

Vectorizing operators close ✕

As the name suggests, we are often interested in "vectorizing" operators so that we may treat them as vectors. Similarly, vectorization pulls down the status of a superoperator (or a channel) to an operator. The way this bijection is established is by defining the identification as follows. Let \(A\) be an operator and \(\mathrm{vec}(A)\) be its vectorization.

\[ A = \sum A_{ij} |i\rangle \langle j| \longleftrightarrow \mathrm{vec}(A) = \sum A_{ij} |i\rangle |j\rangle^* \]

The reason this convention is called row-vectorization is because it stacks the rows first. As an example:

\[ \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2\\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix} \;\longleftrightarrow\; \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix} \]

Denote the normalized vector as \(|A) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{d}} \mathrm{vec}(A)\), where \(d= \mathrm{Tr}\ I\), the dimension of the Hilbert space. A number of simple to prove identities follow, two of which are:

\[ (A | B) = \frac{1}{d} \mathrm{vec}(A)^\dagger \mathrm{vec}(B)= \frac{1}{d}\mathrm{Tr}(A^\dagger B) \] \[ \mathrm{vec}(A O B) = (A \otimes B^T) (\mathrm{vec}(O)) \]

The usual Frobenius norm of a matrix is then given as \(||A||_F = \sqrt{(A|A)} = \sum _{ij} \sqrt {|A_{ij}|^2}\).

Krylov Subspace

Onto the interesting stuff! Recall the Heisenberg equation of motion for an observable \(O\)

\[ \frac{\partial O}{\partial t} = i[H, O] \]

Define the Liouvillian superoperator \(\mathcal{L}\) that acts on operators as \(\mathcal{L}(.) = [H,.]\). Then we may rewrite the preceding equation as

\[ \frac{\partial O}{\partial t} = i\mathcal{L}O \]

In case of a time-independent Hamiltonian, the above equation may be formally solved to obtain (using the BCH formula)

\[ O(t) = \mathrm{e}^{iHt} O \mathrm{e}^{-iHt} = O\ +\ it[H,O]\ +\ \frac{(it)^2}{2}[H,[H,O]]\ +\ ...\ = O\ +\ it \mathcal{L}O\ +\ \frac{(it)^2}{2} \mathcal{L}^2O\ +\ ... \]

or, more succinctly,

\[ O(t) = \mathrm{e}^{it \mathcal{L}} O \]

What does this tell us? At \(t=0\), \(O(t) = O\), but as time passes the contributions due to \(\mathcal{L}O\), \(\mathcal{L}^2O\), ... add up too. The operator explores this "Krylov" subspace spanned by \(\{O,\ \mathcal{L}O,\ \mathcal{L}^2O, ...\}\).

What can we deduce about this basis? Is it orthogonal or normalized? It can be shown that it is neither▸, and therefore maybe we can do better. Recall that the oldest trick in the book to orthonormalize a basis is to use the celebrated Gram-Schmidt procedure.

Checking orthogonality of the first two vectors close ✕

\((O|\mathcal{L}O) = (O|[H,O]) = (O|HO) - (O|OH) = \frac{1}{d}[\mathrm{Tr} (O^\dagger HO) - \mathrm{Tr} (O^\dagger OH)] = \frac{1}{d}[\mathrm{Tr} (O^2 H) - \mathrm{Tr} (O^2 H)] = 0\), where we have used the fact that \(O\) is an observable, and thus Hermitian. Wait! Did we not just prove orthogonality? Well not exactly, because for the entire basis to be orthogonal we would need \((O|\mathcal{L}^2O)\) to vanish as well. Check for yourself that this is indeed not the case!

Lanczos Algorithm

Note that the Liouvillian is Hermitian with respect to the inner product we have been using, i.e. \((A| \mathcal{L} B) = (\mathcal{L} A| B)\), or in vectorized notation \(\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}^\dagger\).

This is a rather nice property to have, because it means we can use \(\mathcal{L}\) to build ourselves a proper orthonormal basis out of the messy set \(\{O, \mathcal{L}O, \mathcal{L}^2O, ...\}\), using essentially the same Gram-Schmidt idea I hinted at earlier. This particular flavour of Gram-Schmidt, tailored to the Liouvillian, has its own name: the Lanczos algorithm. Here's how it goes. Set \(|O_{-1}) := 0\) and \(b_0 := 0\) as bookkeeping conventions, and take \(|O_0) := |O)\) to be our (normalized, Hermitian) starting operator. Then construct the following:

\[ |A_1) = \mathcal{L}|O_0), \qquad b_1 = \| A_1 \|, \qquad |O_1) = |A_1)/b_1, \]

and for every subsequent step \(n>1\),

\[ |A_n) = \mathcal{L}|O_{n-1}) - b_{n-1}|O_{n-2}), \qquad b_n = \|A_n\|, \qquad |O_n) = |A_n)/b_n. \]

Notice the recipe: apply \(\mathcal{L}\), subtract off the piece along the previous basis vector, and normalize. At each step you only ever need to subtract off the immediately preceding vector, not the entire history, which is a nice simplification compared to a generic Gram-Schmidt procedure and is really a consequence of \(\mathcal{L}\) being Hermitian▸.

Why the recursion only reaches back one step close ✕

This follows from \(\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}^\dagger\). Suppose we've built \(\{|O_0), ..., |O_{n-1})\}\) orthonormally. Consider \((O_m|\mathcal{L}|O_{n-1})\) for some \(m < n-2\). By Hermiticity this equals \((\mathcal{L}O_m|O_{n-1})\), and \(\mathcal{L}O_m\) is by construction a combination of \(\{|O_0), ..., |O_{m+1})\}\) only, all of which are orthogonal to \(|O_{n-1})\) since \(m+1 < n-1\). Hence \((O_m|\mathcal{L}|O_{n-1}) = 0\) whenever \(|m - (n-1)| > 1\), and the only surviving overlaps of \(\mathcal{L}|O_{n-1})\) are along \(|O_{n-2})\), \(|O_{n-1})\), and \(|O_n)\). The diagonal piece \((O_{n-1}|\mathcal{L}|O_{n-1})\) vanishes too, by the same Hermiticity argument we used earlier to show \((O|\mathcal{L}O) = 0\). That's why the recursion in step 4 only needs to subtract the previous vector, and it's also why, once you write out the matrix elements \((O_m|\mathcal{L}|O_n)\), you get something tridiagonal, all zeros except for the \(b_n\)'s sitting just off the main diagonal.

This process can't go on forever. It stops once you hit some \(n = D_K\) for which \(\|A_n\| = 0\), meaning \(\mathcal{L}|O_{n-1})\) has nothing new left to offer, it's already fully expressible in terms of the basis you've built so far. This \(D_K\) is called the Krylov dimension, and the resulting ordered set \(\{|O_0), |O_1), ..., |O_{D_K - 1})\}\) is the Krylov basis. Also, purely as a word of caution if you ever try to code this up: the algorithm is numerically finicky. Rounding errors creep in and orthogonality degrades after a surprisingly small number of steps, so in practice people lean on reorthogonalization tricks to keep things honest. This changes the algorithm quite a bit, but for a first introduction this discussion is sufficient. See the Jupyter notebook attached to see what I mean!

How big can \(D_K\) get? Since the Krylov space lives inside the full operator space, which has dimension \(d^2\), you'd think \(D_K \leq d^2\). It turns out you can do a little better: \(D_K\) may be bounded as \(1 \le D_K \le d^2 - d + 1\). Chaotic systems tend to typically saturate this bound, but note that integrable systems could too. The value of \(D_K\) alone doesn't cleanly distinguish chaos from integrability.

Once you have the basis, the whole point was to write \(\mathcal{L}\) compactly. Sandwiching the recursion relation between basis vectors, you find

\[ \mathcal{L}|O_n) = b_n |O_{n-1}) + b_{n+1}|O_{n+1}), \]

which says that \(\mathcal{L}\), in the Krylov basis, is a tridiagonal matrix, with the \(b_n\)'s as its only nonzero entries. So it looks something like

\[ \mathcal{L} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & b_{1} & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ b_{1} & 0 & b_{2} & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & b_{2} & 0 & \ddots & 0 \\ \vdots & \ddots& \ddots & \ddots & b_{D_K-1} \\ 0 & \cdots& 0 & b_{D_K-1} & 0 \end{pmatrix}. \]

Note that in the more general case when \(O\) is not Hermitian, this matrix has diagonal entries too. So we now have a sparse and clean matrix representation of the Liouvillian. But things can get more interesting...

The Hopping Problem

Here's where things get fun. Since \(\{|O_n)\}\) is a basis, we can expand the full time-evolved operator in it. Writing \(|O(t)) = \sum_n i^n \phi_n(t) |O_n)\) (the factors of \(i^n\) are there just to keep the \(\phi_n\)'s real, since \(O(t)\) is Hermitian while \(|O_n)\) generally isn't), and plugging into \(\partial_t |O(t)) = i\mathcal{L}|O(t))\) along with the tridiagonal form of \(\mathcal{L}\) above, you get a neat difference-differential equation▸:

\[ \dot\phi_n(t) = b_n \phi_{n-1}(t) - b_{n+1}\phi_{n+1}(t), \]

with \(\phi_{-1}(t) = 0\) and \(\phi_n(0) = \delta_{n0}\).

Deriving the hopping equation close ✕

To see where this comes from, start from \(\partial_t|O(t)) = i\mathcal{L}|O(t))\) and expand both sides in the Krylov basis. The left side gives \(\sum_n i^n \dot\phi_n(t)|O_n)\). For the right side, use \(|O(t)) = \sum_m i^m \phi_m(t)|O_m)\) and apply \(\mathcal{L}|O_m) = b_m|O_{m-1}) + b_{m+1}|O_{m+1})\) term by term, then relabel the sum so everything is collected against \(|O_n)\). Matching coefficients of \(|O_n)\) on both sides and cancelling the common factor of \(i^n\) gives exactly the stated recursion.

What this equation is really telling you is that the messy many-body operator growth problem has been mapped onto something quite simple: a single particle hopping along a 1D chain, where site \(n\) represents the Krylov basis vector \(|O_n)\), and \(b_n\), \(b_{n+1}\) are the hopping rates to the left and right neighbours. The particle starts pinned at site \(0\) (since \(\phi_n(0) = \delta_{n0}\)) and, as time goes on, spreads outward along the chain according to how large the Lanczos coefficients \(b_n\) are. All the complicated dynamics of \(\mathcal{L}\) has been repackaged into this one sequence of numbers, \(\{b_n\}\). To visualize this, check out the Jupyter notebook attached!

Because time evolution is unitary, the norm of \(O(t)\) is preserved, so \(\sum_n |\phi_n(t)|^2 = 1\) for all \(t\). This lets you read \(|\phi_n(t)|^2\) as a genuine probability, the probability of finding your hopping particle at site \(n\) at time \(t\), or equivalently, of the operator's growth having reached "distance" \(n\) into the Krylov chain. Well if this is a probability distribution, maybe looking at its moments could be insightful.

Krylov Complexity

Given this hopping-particle picture, it's very natural to ask: on average, how far has the particle travelled? That's precisely what the Krylov complexity captures. It's defined as the mean position of the particle in the Krylov chain,

\[ K(t) = \sum_n n\, |\phi_n(t)|^2. \]

At \(t = 0\) the particle sits at site \(0\), so \(K(0) = 0\): the operator hasn't grown at all yet. As time progresses and the wavefunction \(\phi_n(t)\) leaks into higher sites, \(K(t)\) increases, quantifying how far the operator has spread through the Krylov basis, i.e. how "complex" it has become. This is the sense in which \(K(t)\) earns the name Krylov complexity: a genuinely simple operator (like \(O\) itself) sits at low \(n\), while operators that have picked up increasingly intricate, nested-commutator structure sit at high \(n\), and \(K(t)\) tracks the drift from one to the other. Such an object has turned out to be a very useful diagnostic of the dynamics of the underlying problem. Particularly for chaotic dynamics in \(d>1\), \(b_n \sim \alpha n\) and \(K(t) \sim \mathrm{e}^{2 \alpha t}\). Indeed, this is indicative of how strongly scrambling chaotic dynamics can get.

As we come close to the end of this piece, let's quickly recap what we have seen till now. We began by solving the Heisenberg equation of motion for an observable and saw that we could express the solution as a linear combination of non-orthonormal operators that look like \(\{O,\ \mathcal{L}O,\ \mathcal{L}^2O, ...\}\). We then orthonormalized this set to obtain the Krylov basis \(\{|O_0), |O_1), ..., |O_{n - 1})\}\) with the defining relation \(\mathcal{L}|O_n) = b_n |O_{n-1}) + b_{n+1}|O_{n+1})\) that also gave us the Krylov coefficients \(\{b_n\}\). Expanding a general time evolved operator in this orthonormal Krylov basis is useful because the time-dependent coefficients obey nice hopping dynamics, with rates governed by \(\{b_n\}\). Lastly, we defined the mean position of the "hopping particle" as the Krylov complexity. Higher moments also capture some dynamical information, though we omit their discussion here.

Krylov methods in quantum dynamics bring deeper understanding beyond the standard paradigm of analysis techniques. In the case of quantum chaos, it adds onto the list of other diagnostics such as the Spectral Form Factor and Random Matrix Theory spectra. With its much more recent extension to analyzing open quantum systems, it has only become a much more powerful tool. Some recent work also explores Krylov complexity in the context of holography, but honestly I don't understand it (yet!).
