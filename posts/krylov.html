Quantum Chaos

Krylov Complexity

June 2026 12 min read

How do quantum operators grow? If you evolve an operator in time through a Hamiltonian, one is often interested in characterizing how complex this evolution is. Are there any patterns of growth we can find? Quantum dynamics, with its vast richness, has recently benefitted from adopting a rather old perspective on matrices and linear algebra, dating back to the 1930s. In this article, I seek to elucidate the so-called Krylov subspace methods in the context of quantum mechanics. We will see that these techniques allow us to quantify the Krylov complexity of the evolution of an operator. With wide applications in fields ranging from open quantum systems to holography, Krylov complexity has been found to provide deep insights and shows consistent universality across all regimes of dynamics, from the integrable to the completely chaotic.

Schrödinger's equation, i∂t|ψ⟩ = Ĥ|ψ⟩, governs the evolution of quantum states |ψ⟩ under a Hamiltonian Ĥ. For a time-independent Hamiltonian the solution can formally be written as |ψ(t)⟩ = Û|ψ(0)⟩ with Û = exp(−iĤt), and the expectation value of any observable Ô then picks up a piece of time-dependence that we can shift entirely onto the operator itself: this is the Heisenberg picture, where Ô(t) = eiĤt Ô e−iĤt▸. Both pictures give exactly the same physical predictions, but the Heisenberg picture turns out to be the natural language for everything that follows.

From states to operators close ✕

Start from the formal solution |ψ(t)⟩ = e−iĤt|ψ(0)⟩. The expectation value of an observable Ô at time t is

⟨ψ(t)|Ô|ψ(t)⟩ = ⟨ψ(0)| eiĤt Ô e−iĤt |ψ(0)⟩

The right-hand side is just the fixed initial state sandwiching a new, time-dependent operator. Defining

Ô(t) := eiĤt Ô e−iĤt

lets us write ⟨ψ(t)|Ô|ψ(t)⟩ = ⟨ψ(0)|Ô(t)|ψ(0)⟩: the state is frozen at its initial value while all of the time evolution has been absorbed into the operator. This is the Heisenberg picture, entirely equivalent to the Schrödinger picture, just with the burden of evolution moved from kets to operators.

My goal is to leave you with a concrete understanding of the ideas I mention, and to keep it accessible I may gloss over some gory details. But for the interested reader, I have added drop-downs wherever you may want some more clarity. This way, I hope to cater to a larger audience.

Vectorization

Before we begin, a quick heads-up on the idea of vectorization, which will be central to our discussion. Vectorization lets us treat operators as ordinary vectors, equipped with a matching inner product and a short list of useful identities▸. Those already comfortable with it can skip straight past the drop-down; I'll be using the row-vectorization convention throughout.

Vectorizing operators close ✕

Vectorization pulls the status of a superoperator (or channel) down to that of an ordinary operator, via a bijection between operators and vectors. If A is an operator, write it as A = Σ Aij |i⟩⟨j|; its vectorization is defined by

A = Σ Aij |i⟩⟨j|   ⟷   vec(A) = Σ Aij |i⟩|j⟩*

This is called row-vectorization because it stacks the rows of the matrix first, e.g.

⎛1  2⎞         (1  2  3  4)
⎝3  4⎠   ⟷

Denote the normalized version as |A) = vec(A)/√d, where d = Tr I is the dimension of the Hilbert space. A couple of simple identities follow directly:

(A|B) = vec(A)†vec(B)/d = Tr(A†B)/d
vec(AOB) = (A ⊗ BT) vec(O)

The usual Frobenius norm is then ‖A‖F = √(A|A) = Σij |Aij|, recovered as a special case of this inner product.

The Krylov subspace

Onto the interesting part. Recall the Heisenberg equation of motion for an observable O, 

∂O/∂t = i[H, O]

Define the Liouvillian superoperator ℒ that acts on operators as ℒ(·) = [H, ·]. The equation of motion becomes ∂O/∂t = iℒO, and for a time-independent Hamiltonian this can formally be solved with the BCH formula: 

O(t) = eiHt O e−iHt = O + it[H,O] + (it)²/2 [H,[H,O]] + ...
     = O + it·ℒO + (it)²/2·ℒ²O + ...
     = eitℒ O

What does this tell us? At t = 0, O(t) = O, but as time passes the contributions from ℒO, ℒ²O, ... start adding up too. The operator explores this "Krylov" subspace, spanned by {O, ℒO, ℒ²O, ...}.

What can we say about this basis — is it orthogonal, or normalized? It turns out to be neither, so perhaps we can do better. The first two vectors, O and ℒO, are in fact orthogonal — but that pattern doesn't extend to the rest of the set▸. The oldest trick in the book for fixing an un-orthonormal basis is, of course, Gram-Schmidt.

Checking orthogonality by hand close ✕

Compute the overlap directly, using that O is an observable and hence Hermitian:

(O|ℒO) = (O|[H,O]) = (O|HO) − (O|OH)
       = [Tr(O†HO) − Tr(O†OH)]/d
       = [Tr(O²H) − Tr(O²H)]/d = 0

So O and ℒO are orthogonal. But this doesn't mean the whole basis is orthogonal — for that we'd also need (O|ℒ²O) to vanish, and a quick check shows it generally does not. So the set {O, ℒO, ℒ²O, ...} is linearly independent but not an orthonormal basis, which is exactly the gap Gram-Schmidt is built to close.

The Lanczos algorithm

Note that the Liouvillian is Hermitian with respect to the inner product we've been using, i.e. (A|ℒB) = (ℒA|B), or in vectorized notation ℒ = ℒ†. This is a genuinely nice property, because it lets us turn the messy set {O, ℒO, ℒ²O, ...} into a proper orthonormal basis using essentially the same Gram-Schmidt idea, just tailored specifically to ℒ. This flavour of Gram-Schmidt has its own name: the Lanczos algorithm.

Here's how it goes. Set |O−1) := 0 and b0 := 0 as bookkeeping conventions, and take |O0) := |O) to be our normalized, Hermitian starting operator. Then construct: 

|A1) = ℒ|O0),   b1 = ‖A1‖,   |O1) = |A1)/b1

and, for every subsequent step n > 1,

|An) = ℒ|On−1) − bn−1|On−2),   bn = ‖An‖,   |On) = |An)/bn

Notice the recipe: apply ℒ, subtract off the piece along the previous basis vector, and normalize. At each step you only ever need to subtract off the immediately preceding vector, not the entire history — a nice simplification over a generic Gram-Schmidt procedure, and really a direct consequence of ℒ being Hermitian▸.

Why the recursion only reaches back one step close ✕

Suppose we've built {|O0), ..., |On−1)} orthonormally, and consider (Om|ℒ|On−1) for some m < n − 2. By Hermiticity of ℒ, this equals (ℒOm|On−1). But ℒOm is, by construction, only a combination of {|O0), ..., |Om+1)}, all of which are orthogonal to |On−1) whenever m + 1 < n − 1. Hence

(Om|ℒ|On−1) = 0   whenever   |m − (n−1)| > 1

so the only surviving overlaps of ℒ|On−1) are along |On−2), |On−1), and |On). The diagonal piece (On−1|ℒ|On−1) vanishes too, by the same Hermiticity argument used earlier to show (O|ℒO) = 0. That's why the recursion only needs to subtract the previous vector — and it's also why, once you write out all the matrix elements (Om|ℒ|On), the result is tridiagonal: zero everywhere except for the bn's sitting just off the main diagonal.

This process can't go on forever. It stops once you reach some n = DK for which ‖An‖ = 0, meaning ℒ|On−1) has nothing new left to offer — it's already fully expressible in the basis built so far. This DK is the Krylov dimension, and the resulting ordered set {|O0), ..., |ODK−1)} is the Krylov basis. As a word of caution if you ever code this up: the algorithm is numerically finicky, rounding errors creep in and orthogonality degrades after a surprisingly small number of steps, so in practice people lean on reorthogonalization tricks to keep things honest — see the Jupyter notebook attached for a taste of this.

How big can DK get? Since the Krylov space lives inside the full operator space, of dimension d², you might expect DK ≤ d². It turns out you can do a little better: 1 ≤ DK ≤ d² − d + 1. Chaotic systems tend to saturate this bound, but integrable systems can too — the value of DK alone doesn't cleanly distinguish chaos from integrability.

Once you have the basis, the point was to write ℒ compactly. Sandwiching the recursion relation between basis vectors gives 

ℒ|On) = bn|On−1) + bn+1|On+1)

which says that ℒ, in the Krylov basis, is a tridiagonal matrix with the bn's as its only nonzero entries: 

     ⎛ 0    b₁   0    ⋯    0  ⎞
     ⎜ b₁   0    b₂   ⋱    ⋮  ⎟
ℒ  = ⎜ 0    b₂   0    ⋱    0  ⎟
     ⎜ ⋮    ⋱    ⋱    ⋱  bDK−1 ⎟
     ⎝ 0    ⋯    0  bDK−1  0  ⎠

(In the more general case where O is not Hermitian, this matrix picks up diagonal entries too.) Either way, we now have a sparse, clean matrix representation of the Liouvillian. But things get more interesting still.

The hopping problem

Here's where things get fun. Since {|On)} is a basis, we can expand the full time-evolved operator in it. Write |O(t)) = Σn in φn(t) |On) (the factors of in are there just to keep the φn's real, since O(t) is Hermitian while |On) generally isn't). Plugging this into ∂t|O(t)) = iℒ|O(t)) and using the tridiagonal form of ℒ gives a neat difference-differential equation▸: 

φ̇n(t) = bn φn−1(t) − bn+1 φn+1(t)

with φ−1(t) = 0 and φn(0) = δn0.

Deriving the recursion close ✕

Start from ∂t|O(t)) = iℒ|O(t)) and expand both sides in the Krylov basis. The left side gives Σn in φ̇n(t) |On). For the right side, substitute |O(t)) = Σm im φm(t) |Om) and apply ℒ|Om) = bm|Om−1) + bm+1|Om+1) term by term, then relabel the sums so everything is collected against a single |On). Matching coefficients of |On) on both sides and cancelling the common factor of in gives exactly the stated recursion for φ̇n(t).

What this equation is really telling you is that the messy, many-body operator growth problem has been mapped onto something quite simple: a single particle hopping along a 1D chain, where site n represents the Krylov basis vector |On), and bn, bn+1 are the hopping rates to the left and right neighbours. The particle starts pinned at site 0 (since φn(0) = δn0) and, as time goes on, spreads outward along the chain according to how large the Lanczos coefficients bn are. All of the complicated dynamics of ℒ has been repackaged into this one sequence of numbers, {bn}. To visualize this, check out the Jupyter notebook attached!

Because time evolution is unitary, the norm of O(t) is preserved, so Σn |φn(t)|² = 1 for all t. This lets you read |φn(t)|² as a genuine probability: the probability of finding the hopping particle at site n at time t, or equivalently, of the operator's growth having reached "distance" n into the Krylov chain. If this is a probability distribution, maybe its moments are worth a look.

Krylov complexity

Given this hopping-particle picture, it's very natural to ask: on average, how far has the particle travelled? That's precisely what the Krylov complexity captures. It's defined as the mean position of the particle along the Krylov chain, 

K(t) = Σn n |φn(t)|²

At t = 0 the particle sits at site 0, so K(0) = 0: the operator hasn't grown at all yet. As time progresses and the wavefunction φn(t) leaks into higher sites, K(t) increases, quantifying how far the operator has spread through the Krylov basis — how "complex" it has become. This is the sense in which K(t) earns the name Krylov complexity: a genuinely simple operator (like O itself) sits at low n, while operators that have picked up increasingly intricate, nested-commutator structure sit at high n, and K(t) tracks the drift from one to the other. This has turned out to be a very useful diagnostic of the underlying dynamics. Particularly for chaotic dynamics in d > 1, bn ~ αn and K(t) ~ e2αt — indicative of just how strongly scrambling chaotic dynamics can get.

As we come to the end of this piece, let's quickly recap. We began by solving the Heisenberg equation of motion for an observable and saw that its solution could be expressed as a combination of non-orthonormal operators {O, ℒO, ℒ²O, ...}. We then orthonormalized this set to obtain the Krylov basis {|O0), ..., |On−1)} via the defining relation ℒ|On) = bn|On−1) + bn+1|On+1), which also handed us the Lanczos coefficients {bn}. Expanding a general time-evolved operator in this orthonormal Krylov basis is useful precisely because the time-dependent coefficients obey a simple hopping dynamics, with rates set by {bn}. Finally, we defined the mean position of this "hopping particle" as the Krylov complexity. Higher moments capture further dynamical information too, though we omit that discussion here.

Krylov methods in quantum dynamics bring a deeper understanding beyond the standard paradigm of analysis techniques. In the study of quantum chaos, it adds to the list of other diagnostics such as the spectral form factor and random matrix theory spectra. With its more recent extension to open quantum systems, it has only become a more powerful tool. Some recent work also explores Krylov complexity in the context of holography — but honestly, I don't understand it yet.
