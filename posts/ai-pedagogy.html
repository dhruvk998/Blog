AI & Education

Thought, Pedagogy, and Artificial Intelligence

18th October 2026 6 min read

If LLMs know everything, are more patient while explaining, and are much quicker to answer, why should we students even bother going to classes? A more grave formulation could be: what is the value of knowledge and its dissemination by humans in a world where information and expertise has never been more accessible? For many people, each term in this question has a variety of meanings. This brief essay is therefore a personal account of my understanding of AI and education, and what a future built on today’s premise might look like.

As a physics student, I often discuss mathematical arguments on the fly with my professors and friends. Either this could be during classes when it is slightly one-sided, or to discuss an idea or a realization someone had, which often requires active effort. The latter is almost like knitting: first we sit together with a vague idea of what to construct in our own heads, then we get to work by explicitly crafting what we imagined so that the other may understand what you meant, there are some mistakes and some obstacles, we go back and forth but finally there is a product! A flow of thought, woven neatly as best as we could, is an object worth treasuring. It is precisely this process, with or without another human, that I yearn for in my education. A process which may not even have an immediate conclusion, but one that hosts joy in solely its existence.

When the British colonialists assumed control over India in the 18th century, the traditional weavers of fine muslin and cloth suffered a crushing blow. They had no way to keep up with the affordability and mass production of Victorian England. As if that wasn’t enough, they broke the charkhas of our weavers to ensure that the mighty but delicate textile industry of India was utterly crippled. I claim that we are observing the same phenomenon with AI today.

Let’s be a bit more direct now. Researchers or students whose primary goal is to simply obtain an answer to a given question benefit mightily from harnessing the capabilities of LLMs. Typically such an engineer cares more about whether a solution is efficient and reproducible, and innovation is not something they always optimize for. For such people, the efficiency and accessibility of AI is a boon. On the other hand, these are exactly the properties that are considered unwelcome by the other group — those who prefer playing around with the problem in their own time and on their own pace. They don’t like how quickly and accurately the LLM responds, because they fear that outsourcing the process of discovery might gradually atrophy the very mental faculties required to think independently — as though AI were “breaking their charkhas”. As kids, they were revolted by the annoying, nerdy kid who would spoil the answer to a math problem even without having been asked. These are the people who would have bought the more expensive Indian cloth, with all its cost and imperfections. The former group would believe in the more pragmatic view of buying cloth at an affordable price to achieve what cloth is meant to do: to cover and protect. To be clear, no group is decidedly better than the other. To be more practical is as bad or as good as to be more romantic. Still, I think I belong to the second group. That doesn’t mean I am revolted by LLMs. On the contrary, I have used it extensively to code for me, to get solutions to problem sets and even use it to understand difficult concepts. To return to the question posed in the beginning: why then do I still bother showing up to classes if I can learn everything using AI?

Easy answer: empathy. Currently, your learning solely through AI depends strongly on the quality and depth of your prompts. Good for you if you understand the subject matter well to have context, but not so good if you’re a complete beginner. A professor on the other hand, can know from your face and your eyes just how lost you are. The quality of a human answer is not limited by the statement of the “prompt” you asked. An empathetic answer is thus a culmination of a) an understanding of the questioner’s mental frame and intellectual ability, b) years of experience of knowing common pitfalls faced by students, and c) the ability to communicate clearly.

It is true that I can probably acquire the same knowledge the professors teach in half the time by myself using AI, but another reason to attend a class is to understand the professor’s unique experience of the subject. No two instructors will have the same way of teaching, and their academic interests often colour the content of their teaching in interesting ways. Is it not worthwhile to enjoy multiple schools of thought, whether it be on physics or philosophy? Probably today’s LLMs aren’t great at providing more than one motivation/idea.

The day empathy and experience becomes natural to AI is the day I shall accept the possibility of AGI. But till then, what would the future leading up to such a day even look like?

(We cannot go an entire blogpost without discussing OpenAI’s recent mathematical advances. Where do we even begin!)

When the Navier-Stokes solution came out, the world fell silent. Even though it (and its debated authorship) is still under review, even the possibility that LLMs are able to solve Millennium problems is a complete game-changer. How long until each of the other problems fall to AI? Will the pillars of mathematics fall so soon? The Riemann Hypothesis has possible connections to quantum chaos, and I want to explore this link before it is solved! Recently a solution to the quasi-RH was found by OpenAI as well, which certainly got me palpitating.

But if this one big scare wasn’t enough, OpenAI decided to release solutions to 700+ open math problems, each of them solved using LLMs (recall the annoying, nerdy kid). Needless to say, all of these events have shaken the mathematical community to the core. We seem to be sinking in a deluge of AI generated proofs. It takes a huge amount of time to verify even a single paper. Now there will be even less time to absorb these ideas and inculcate them into our mathematical arsenal. My question: what happens when new AI-written papers start citing older AI-written papers? One will lead to two, two will lead to four. We will be stuck in exponentially large numbers of incomprehensible AI-generated “mathematics”. Could we avoid this by enforcing AI-written papers to always cite/use human-written papers? This has an obvious loophole: how will the LLMs verify if a paper was completely human-written or not? Perhaps the authors did use AI but did not acknowledge. Or worse: they used the results of AI-written papers without verifying the results to write their “human-written” paper. That would again lead to the same phenomenon of exponentially large build-up, albeit in a second-order, indirect fashion. We must ask ourselves then — what do we plan to do with this possible build-up of “Artificial Mathematics”? Do we let it grow on its own while we continue with business as usual, thus making it seem like there was no threat in the first place? Or do we take it seriously and aim to verify each AI-written paper, no matter how daunting the entire task may seem?

Motivated by these developments, I conjecture a future where AI/AGI completely solves all of physics definitively. The solution would be right there, completely within reach. The only issue is that it is unmotivated and extremely incomprehensible. To be a physicist would then mean to devote yourself to deciphering this solution, and building a more digestible framework of physics for humans. Will pedagogy become relevant again?

I frankly don’t have answers to many of the questions I have posed. But these issues have been bugging me since a very long time, and I finally got to pen these down. We are living in historic times. First it was physical labour that was eased with the rise of the Industrial Revolution. What happens now that our mental labour is eased in the Intelligence Age? Society and economics is going to transform again, and we best be ready for it.
