Hamiltonian Chaos

Chirikov, Nietzsche and Hamiltonian Chaos

3rd August 2025 5 min read

“And if you gaze for long into an abyss, the abyss gazes also into you.” ~ Nietzsche

It is not easy to find this particular abyss, but once you do, it stares hauntingly right back at you, unblinking and obstinate. A surprised eye smack in the middle, holding your gaze as if you don’t belong here. This is not a picture of outer space, nor is it a flow of some actual fluid, but a far less obvious detail of a system you see everywhere all around you, and a physicist’s dear friend — the simple pendulum.

Story time! In the ’60s, Boris Chirikov was playing around with charges and magnets, and his equations were decent looking. Naively, one would expect simple enough motion, but he was astounded to see that his charges bounced around almost randomly. This was going to be Chirikov’s first encounter with the untamed beast, we know as chaos.

Of course, any motion at this level is (in principle) guided by the laws of quantum mechanics but it is not (in practice) very easily solved for because the math gets very complicated very quickly. Chirikov speaks of ‘constructive physics’ where through experiments and simulations, one constructs a relatively simple mathematical model. These models are technically approximations, but at that time one could do worse by making linear approximations (simple but uninteresting), as opposed to Chirikov who wished to make nonlinear models (where all the fun lies). His monograph of ~240 pages kicked off a revolution in a field of physics that technical people like to call Hamiltonian Chaos. JB Taylor and John Greene are two other big names, with critical contributions to this field and particularly to this problem. Without further ado, let’s create the abyss now, shall we?

Imagine you’re blind, drifting in the void of space, and you have a pendulum — suspended, slightly deflected, but otherwise frozen in zero gravity. You also have a magical button. Each time you press it, gravity switches on for the briefest instant. In that moment, the pendulum jerks in response and your vision is restored just long enough to record its state: the angle from vertical \(x\), and its momentum \(p\), both altered by that sudden gravitational nudge. Then back to blindness.

You keep pressing the button every \(T\) seconds, obsessively noting down the evolving values of \((x, p)\) each time. Different initial conditions, different trajectories. Once you’ve collected enough data and finally return to Earth, you plot all those points. If you’ve done it right, the abyss shall open its eye to you.

Of course, this method is absolutely ridiculous, but I would like you to have the above picture in mind when I say that our goal is to sample the pendulum’s state \((x, p)\) after every “kick” and then create a phase portrait of its dynamics. Why? Because this process yields precisely the equations Chirikov obtained when confining plasma in magnetic traps. For the ones dying to see some math, here you go:

A general Hamiltonian with periodic kicking looks like

\[ H(x,p,t) = H_0(x,p) + V(x)\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} \delta(t - nT). \]

By Hamilton’s equations,

\[ \dot{x} = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p}, \qquad \dot{p} = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial x}. \]

For the pendulum,

\[ H(x,p,t) = \frac{p^2}{2} + K_0 \cos x \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} \delta(t - nT). \]

The next part of “sampling” this continuous time system to arrive at a discrete time system▸ is tricky, and will need a little more analysis but effectively, one arrives at the following (after rescaling):

\[ \begin{aligned} p_{n+1} &= p_n + K \sin x_n, \\ x_{n+1} &= x_n + p_{n+1}. \end{aligned} \]
From the kicked pendulum to the map close ✕

Between kicks the delta functions vanish, so \(H = p^2/2\) and Hamilton’s equations give \(\dot p = 0\), \(\dot x = p\): the pendulum simply drifts at constant momentum. At a kick, \(t = nT\), we have

\[ \dot p = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial x} = K_0 \sin x \; \delta(t - nT), \]

while \(\dot x = p\) stays finite, so \(x\) does not jump across the kick (the kick is instantaneous) but \(p\) does. Integrating \(\dot p\) across the kick gives a jump \(\Delta p = K_0 \sin x\).

Sample the state \((x_n, p_n)\) just before the \(n\)th kick. The kick changes the momentum, and then the pendulum drifts for a time \(T\):

\[ p_{n+1} = p_n + K_0 \sin x_n, \qquad x_{n+1} = x_n + T\,p_{n+1}. \]

Now rescale the momentum, \(P_n = T p_n\), and define \(K = K_0 T\). Multiplying the first equation by \(T\) gives

\[ P_{n+1} = P_n + K \sin x_n, \qquad x_{n+1} = x_n + P_{n+1}, \]

which is the map above, with \(P\) renamed \(p\). The three constants \(K_0\), \(T\) and the pendulum’s normalisation have collapsed into the single number \(K = K_0 T\), which is why only one parameter controls everything. Since \(x\) is an angle it is taken mod \(2\pi\). Sampling just after each kick instead gives the same equations in the opposite order, which is one of the equivalent forms mentioned below.

This map, takes input as the angle and momentum after the \(n\)th kick, and maps this to some output angle and momentum after the \((n+1)\)th kick. It is famously known as the standard map, or Chirikov map, or Chirikov-Taylor-Greene map. I would like to bring to your attention several important observations:

  1. The order in which I wrote the equations matters, and you may see different signs or variables anywhere else but all these equations are topologically same and contain the same dynamics.
  2. Only one parameter \(K\) controls all dynamical behaviour.
  3. Since these equations were derived from a Hamiltonian, the dynamics obeys Liouville’s theorem and the Jacobian \(J\) obeys \(\det J = 1\)▸ (try it!).
The Jacobian has unit determinant close ✕

Write the map as \(x' = x + p'\), \(p' = p + K\sin x\). The partial derivatives are \(\partial p'/\partial x = K\cos x\), \(\partial p'/\partial p = 1\), \(\partial x'/\partial x = 1 + K\cos x\) and \(\partial x'/\partial p = 1\), so

\[ J = \frac{\partial (x', p')}{\partial (x, p)} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + K\cos x & 1 \\ K\cos x & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det J = (1 + K\cos x) - K\cos x = 1. \]

Equivalently, the map is a composition of two shears, a kick and a drift, each with unit determinant:

\[ J = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ K\cos x & 1 \end{pmatrix}. \]

\(\det J = 1\) means the map preserves area in the \((x,p)\) plane. This is the discrete-time version of Liouville’s theorem: phase-space blobs can be stretched and folded, but never compressed.

So what really are these “eyes” in the phase space? They represent islands▸ of quasiperiodic (almost periodic) motion, spread out in a sea of chaos where the motion seems erratic and appears as almost randomly distributed points. It was exactly this motion Chirikov observed! For small \(K\) (weak kicking), the phase space maintains most of its regularity, but for large enough \(K\)▸ (strong kicking), chaos takes over. The interactive plot at the top of this page shows the same: drag the slider and watch the eye open and close.

Where the eye comes from close ✕

Take \(x\) and \(p\) mod \(2\pi\). A fixed point needs \(x_{n+1} = x_n\), which forces \(p_{n+1} \equiv 0\), and \(p_{n+1} = p_n\), which forces \(K\sin x_n \equiv 0\). Taking \(p = 0\) this leaves \(\sin x = 0\), so the fixed points are \((x,p) = (0,0)\) and \((\pi,0)\).

Linearising about a fixed point, small deviations evolve with the Jacobian from before. Since \(\det J = 1\), its eigenvalues obey \(\lambda^2 - (\mathrm{tr}\,J)\lambda + 1 = 0\), with

\[ \mathrm{tr}\,J = 2 + K\cos x. \]

At \((0,0)\), \(\mathrm{tr}\,J = 2 + K > 2\) for \(K>0\): the eigenvalues are real, one larger than \(1\) and one smaller, so nearby points are stretched along one direction and squeezed along the other. This hyperbolic point is unstable, and it is where chaos is born.

At \((\pi,0)\), \(\mathrm{tr}\,J = 2 - K\). For \(0 < K < 4\) we have \(|\mathrm{tr}\,J| < 2\), so the eigenvalues are complex phases \(e^{\pm i\theta}\) with \(\cos\theta = 1 - K/2\). Nearby orbits rotate around the fixed point instead of leaving it: this is the elliptic point at the centre of the “eye”, surrounded by quasiperiodic orbits. At \(K = 4\) the trace reaches \(-2\) and this point loses its linear stability. (Showing that the surrounding curves really survive the nonlinearity needs KAM theory; the linearisation only tells you where to look.)

How chaos takes over close ✕

At \(K = 0\) the map is \(p_{n+1} = p_n\), \(x_{n+1} = x_n + p_n\). Momentum never changes, so every orbit lies on a horizontal line \(p = \text{const}\). If \(p/2\pi\) is rational the orbit is periodic, and if it is irrational the orbit fills the line densely: quasiperiodic motion, with no chaos at all.

For small \(K > 0\), the Kolmogorov–Arnold–Moser (KAM) theorem says most of these irrational lines survive, only slightly deformed. The rational ones are destroyed and replaced by chains of islands, with a thin chaotic layer around the hyperbolic points. As \(K\) grows, the islands widen, the chaotic layers thicken and begin to merge.

A sharp threshold is the destruction of the last invariant curve that runs across the whole \(x\) range, the one with golden-mean rotation number. Numerically (Greene’s residue criterion) this happens at \(K_c \approx 0.9716\). Beyond \(K_c\) no such curve remains to confine the momentum, so a single chaotic orbit can wander over a large range of \(p\). You can see the change by sweeping the slider through \(K \approx 1\) in the plot above.

There is a lot more hidden structure, and I haven’t even scratched the surface yet. Indeed, I’m not a master of the field, but I couldn’t help but share this brilliant piece of physics with you. Chaotic maps such as the standard map go deep, but are still classical. It turns out that you can bring in quantum mechanics and have quantum chaos, as if the two were simple enough separately! But quantum chaos is an active field of research and has direct applications in quantum computing. Hopefully, it will become a subject I get to delve into soon.
